How to edit videos

A practical starting guide — plus editing software examples

Video editing is the process of arranging, trimming, combining, and polishing video clips to tell a story, convey information, or simply make the footage more watchable. Whether you're editing a vlog, a short film, a social media clip, or a family memory, the core steps are remarkably similar. Below you'll find a clear path from raw footage to final export — followed by a list of editing software you can try today.

How to edit a video (step by step)

  1. Organize and review your footage. Gather all clips, audio, and graphics in one folder. Watch through your material and note the best takes, key moments, or soundbites. Rename files if helpful — a little order saves hours later.
  2. Import and set up your project. Create a new project in your editing software. Import your media. Set the correct resolution and frame rate (e.g., 1080p at 30 fps or 4K at 24 fps). If you’re working with multiple sources, check that they match.
  3. Build a rough cut. Drag your best clips onto the timeline in the order you want them to appear. Don’t worry about perfection yet — just get the story or structure down. Trim obvious unwanted parts (false starts, dead air).
  4. Refine the edit. Tighten the pacing. Shorten clips so they cut on action or emotion. Add transitions (only if they serve the story — simple cuts are usually best). Adjust the timing of cuts so dialogue or movement feels natural.
  5. Work on audio. Good audio is half the video. Level out the volume between clips, reduce background noise, and add music or sound effects if appropriate. Use fade‑ins/fade‑outs to avoid abrupt audio pops. Many editors include basic noise reduction and EQ tools.
  6. Add titles, graphics, and color correction. Insert titles, lower‑thirds, or captions where needed. Adjust brightness, contrast, and color balance to make footage look consistent. If your software supports LUTs or color wheels, use them subtly.
  7. Final review and export. Watch the entire video from start to finish. Look for awkward cuts, audio glitches, or missing elements. When satisfied, export using a standard preset — H.264 (MP4) is universally compatible. Choose a resolution and bitrate that balances quality and file size (e.g., 1080p at 10–15 Mbps).

Example editing software

There’s no single “best” editor — it depends on your device, budget, and how deep you want to go. Here are four reliable options that cover beginners to professionals.

professional · free

DaVinci Resolve

Industry‑grade editing, color grading, VFX, and audio post‑production — all in one. The free version is incredibly full‑featured; the Studio version adds advanced AI tools and collaboration.

Best for: colorists, indie filmmakers, and anyone wanting pro tools without a subscription.
industry standard

Adobe Premiere Pro

Used in broadcast, film, and online content. Deep integration with After Effects and Photoshop, extensive plugin ecosystem, and very flexible timeline. Subscription‑based.

Best for: professionals, teams, and editors who want seamless Adobe workflow.
easy · cross‑platform

CapCut

Modern, intuitive editor with strong social media features: auto‑captions, trendy templates, background removal, and effects. Free for most features. Desktop and mobile.

Best for: short‑form content, TikTok/Reels, and quick edits with style.
beginner · Apple

iMovie

Pre‑installed on Mac and iOS. Simple drag‑and‑drop interface, basic titles, music, and transitions. Excellent for learning the fundamentals without overwhelm. Completely free.

Best for: beginners, casual projects, and anyone in the Apple ecosystem.

Also worth knowing

  • Final Cut Pro — Apple’s pro editor, optimized for Mac, one‑time purchase.
  • Filmora — beginner‑friendly with lots of effects, cross‑platform.
  • Shotcut — open‑source, completely free, cross‑platform.
  • Kdenlive — open‑source and great for Linux users.

Quick tip: The best way to learn editing is to finish a short project — even 30 seconds. Pick three clips, trim them, add music, and export. You’ll learn more from completing one small video than from hours of tutorials.
